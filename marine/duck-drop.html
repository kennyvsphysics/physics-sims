The Friendly Floatees

On January 10, 1992, a storm knocked containers off the cargo ship Ever Laurel near the International Date Line. One held 28,800 bath toys: yellow ducks, red beavers, blue turtles and green frogs. Beachcombers reported where they washed up, and oceanographers Curtis Ebbesmeyer and Jim Ingraham used those reports to test their computer model of surface currents.

    Long Island connection

    The Gulf Stream carries Florida-warm water north, but it runs well over 100 miles south of Long Island. The water off our beaches is cooler shelf water, partly fed from the north by the Labrador Current.

    In late summer, warm eddies called warm-core rings break off the Gulf Stream and drift toward the shelf, sometimes bringing tropical fish to Long Island waters. Most of these “tropical strays” don’t survive the winter.

    About this model
    • Currents are yearly averages drawn from textbook current maps. Seasonal changes (like the Indian Ocean monsoon currents) are left out.
    • Each duck follows the current under it plus a little random wobble that stands in for eddies and wind.
    • Real Floatees also rode the wind because they float high in the water, so the real ones moved somewhat faster than water alone.
    • A duck that enters the Bering Strait gets frozen into Arctic sea ice. It drifts across the North Pole, which a flat map can't show, and comes out near Greenland 5–7 years later. Ebbesmeyer and Ingraham predicted this route.
    • Temperatures are approximate yearly-average surface values.
